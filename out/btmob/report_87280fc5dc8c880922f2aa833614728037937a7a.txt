About USD 1.5 billion in Bitcoin (18,270 BTC) cannot be frozen. Root cause (as stated by Binance): a compromised engineer’s laptop was used to submit unauthorized withdrawals through the exchange’s signing pipeline; Binance reports no private-key leakage. The delivery method on the laptop remains unpublished. Incident Description On 29 October 2026, public reporting described an active investigation into abnormal cryptocurrency movements associated with exchange wallets labeled as belonging to Binance. Initial estimates of suspicious outflows ranged from approximately USD 3.3 billion to USD 5.8 billion , depending on the blockchain networks, wallet clusters, and transactions included in the analysis. These figures represent third-party estimates of potentially suspicious asset movements rather than confirmed financial losses. The actual amount stolen and the final net financial impact remain undetermined. Public reporting also described temporary withdrawal restrictions and the freezing of substantial stablecoin holdings by token issuers. On-chain analysis linked a set of Ethereum transfers to EIP-7702 account delegation activity that preceded the largest movements. This assessment is based on publicly available information and remains preliminary. Further validation is required to establish the full scope of the incident and confirm the suspected attack mechanism. Technical Analysis Initial Compromise & Infrastructure Breach Compromise Vector: Preliminary investigation confirmed that the incident originated via the compromise of a Binance DevOps engineer’s laptop. investigation indicates that this was a sophisticated, multi-stage campaign. It was not a single isolated breach or a simple phishing incident. The attackers combined a range of techniques, including social engineering by fake recruitment interviews, malicious software downloads, compromised internal AI tooling, endpoint compromise, and an advanced transaction-authorisation method. June 2026 — Supply chain attack. Engineer A from Wallet DevOps downloaded tampered Homebrew installation in order to install a popular AI coding tool after searching for it on a search engine. Binance security team detected the anomaly, replaced the device, and rotated all related credentials as a proactive security action. However, Engineer A’s role and his related colleagues on Binance’s wallet infrastructure team was exposed to the hacker. Throughout 2026, Binance's security team detected and prevented 57 suspected North Korea-linked cyber threat cases. These attempts reflect the persistent threat posed by state-sponsored actors targeting the cryptocurrency industry. The techniques include fraudulent job interviews, malicious software disguised as popular developer tools, compromised software packages and browser extensions, fake video conferencing applications, and social engineering campaigns designed to steal credentials or deploy remote access malware. September — social engineering. Software Engineer B was contacted through a legitimate job-hunting platform by an individual posing as a Project Director. Software Engineer B had a profile on the platform that referenced his experience at Binance. The purported Project Director arranged a first-round interview with a HR representative, followed by a second-round interview with an individual claiming to be the CEO of a fictitious company. In the first round, the purported HR representative conducted a live video interview with Software Engineer B. The interviewer shared a social media account with him, including personal photos and videos posted on the platform, in order to build trust. This was more advanced and deceptive than previously seen social engineering approaches. The second interview was conducted in a popular social chat app, near the end of the interview, Engineer B was instructed to download an “assignment” containing a malicious code repository onto his work computer. The repository contained malicious code that executed during the setup process, initiating a series of unauthorized activities. The malware employed sophisticated obfuscation and evasion techniques, including replacing a legitimate development tool component with a malicious version to execute commands and establish communication with an attacker-controlled external server. The 0-day malware was not detected by the endpoint security protection tools at the time. The attacker also took steps to conceal the malicious activity and remove traces of the compromise, making detection and subsequent investigation more challenging. Privilege Misuse: The attacker leveraged the compromised workstation to access an internal service container, executing unauthorized withdrawal operations directly through the exchange's transaction generation and signing pipelines. Integrity of Private Keys: Binance found no evidence of any cold-storage or hot-wallet private key leakage. Instead, the attacker abused the automated hot-wallet signing infrastructure across multiple heterogeneous chains to generate legitimate, cryptographically valid signatures on attacker-controlled payloads Pre-Operational Staging & Smart Contract Testing Funding: Operational funding commenced approximately 36 hours prior to the theft. On the morning of 28 October 2026, an operator wallet ( 0x7c8601d4645b93cea45f71e118a729332e6b5ca0 ) received three seed transfers from Tornado Cash. Contract Deployment & Dry Runs: Early on 29 October 2026, the operator deployed the primary wallet-sweeping smart contract. The code was iteratively validated across two test wallets between 13:51 and 16:47 UTC, followed by a final rehearsal on a third wallet before routing test funds back to the operator. Approximately four hours after this rehearsal, the payload was installed on Binance’s primary hot wallet Ethereum Vector: EIP-7702 Account Delegation Abuse Ethereum's EIP-7702 standard (implemented in the May 2025 Pectra upgrade) allows an externally owned account (EOA) to temporarily delegate its code execution logic to a designated smart contract address by signing an authorization payload. Nonce-Race Mitigation (50 Pre-Signed Authorizations): Because an active centralized exchange hot wallet continuously processes customer withdrawals, its transaction sequence number (nonce) advances rapidly. To overcome nonce invalidation, the threat actor generated a pre-signed batch of 50 consecutive EIP-7702 authorizations pointing to their malicious sweeper contract. Sweeper Deployment: At 20:20:59 UTC, the operator broadcasted the authorization transaction. One of the 50 consecutive nonces matched the hot wallet's active nonce state, instantly binding the hot wallet ( Binance 14 : 0x28C6c06298d514Db089934071355E5743bf21d60 ) to the malicious sweeper. The sweeper was constructed to accept execution commands exclusively from the operator. Primary Asset Sweep: At 20:24:23 UTC (3 minutes, 24 seconds post-delegation), the operator invoked the sweeper, draining 167,464 ETH and 63 ERC-20 tokens—including 1.615 billion USDT —into the primary collection address ( 0x5227f603c1ee34ce3c06efe6477003bdfccb2f8c ) in a single transaction. Smart Account Drainage & Persistent Interception: The compromised Binance 14 address also served as the authorized executor for a secondary Binance smart account, enabling the attacker to trigger an unauthorized transfer of 823 million USDC . Because the EIP-7702 delegation remained active on-chain, incoming customer deposits were automatically swept across consecutive intervals at 20:41, 20:59, 21:04, 21:44, 22:39, and 23:56 UTC. Secondary Ethereum Hot Wallet Drainage: Simultaneously at 20:24:47 UTC, a secondary hot wallet ( Binance 16 ) was drained of 28,006 ETH , 55.7 million USDT , and 25.9 million RLUSD via direct standard signed transactions without EIP-7702 delegation. Asset Movement and Cross-Chain Impact Ethereum-focused reporting described transfers from the wallets labeled Binance 14 and Binance 16 into a common receiving address, followed by onward movement that may have included asset swaps. The transfers to a shared collection point and subsequent redistribution are the most consistently reported elements of the on-chain activity. Broader claims extended the activity to Bitcoin and Tron, but not every cross-chain transfer can be conclusively linked to a single malicious operation. The various published estimates of suspicious outflows overlap one another and should never be summed into a single total. Threat Actor Attribution Threat Actor Attribution: TraderTraitor / Lazarus Group (DPRK) Assessment: We assess with Moderate Confidence that the threat actor responsible for the 29 October 2026 Binance hot-wallet compromise and unauthorized multi-chain asset exfiltration is TraderTraitor (an elite cyber unit operating under North Korea's Lazarus Group / RGB) Update (31 October 2026): The FBI, together with USSS and IRS-CI, issued a public alert stating that North Korea was responsible for the theft and that the activity is tracked as “TraderTraitor”. The alert states the stolen assets are being converted primarily into ETH and expects further laundering through DeFi and instant-swap services. This government attribution, together with the independent assessments of TRM Labs, Chainalysis and Elliptic, supports raising confidence from Moderate to High. Analytic Basis & Supporting Evidence On-Chain Fund Commingling: Blockchain intelligence investigators observed funds stolen in the Binance exploit directly commingling with proceeds from DPRK's exploit of Bitget from the previous month. Operational Pre-Staging & Infrastructure Setup: Preparation began at least 36 hours prior to the theft, originating with small funding transfers from Tornado Cash to an operator wallet starting on the morning of 28 October 2026. The operator deployed sweeper code, rehearsed execution across three test wallets, and routed funds back before deploying the payload against Binance’s hot wallet. This disciplined multi-stage reconnaissance and staging pattern mirrors prior DPRK-attributed operations, including the February 2025 Bybit heist ($1.5B) and the April 2026 KelpDAO exploit. Burst Execution & Batch Signing Exploitation: The exfiltration leveraged 50 pre-signed EIP-7702 authorizations targeting consecutive transaction nonces on Ethereum, executed simultaneously alongside coordinated signed withdrawals across nine non-EVM and EVM chains within a ~20-minute window. This synchronized, multi-chain signing abuse demonstrates the high organizational capacity characteristic of state-sponsored DPRK actors. Alignment with Established Initial Access Playbooks (Social Engineering & DevOps Targeting): Binance’s preliminary finding that the incident originated via a compromised DevOps engineer’s endpoint strongly aligns with the known initial access tradecraft of TraderTraitor / Lazarus Group. State-sponsored DPRK cyber units systematically deploy sophisticated recruitment-themed social engineering campaigns (e.g., fraudulent job offers, technical interview coding challenges, and trojanized open-source repositories) specifically tailored to compromise engineers, system administrators, and DevOps personnel possessing elevated operational privileges. While the definitive delivery payload on the engineer's device remains under investigation, the exploitation of an infrastructure engineer’s workstation to bridge into internal production containers closely mirrors historical DPRK campaigns against digital asset platforms. Implications and Recommendations The following defensive and investigative actions were recommended: Audit EIP-7702 authorizations and delegated code on high-value externally owned accounts. Investigate HSM/KMS systems, wallet signing systems, approval workflows, and privileged administrative actions. Apply transfer caps, destination allowlists, independent approvals, and anomaly monitoring. Monitor for new delegate contracts, unusual token sweeps, stablecoin swaps, and mixer-related flows. Prepare rapid containment and issuer-coordination processes, and reconcile assets against liabilities. Screen and, where appropriate, block transactions with or derived from the addresses in the FBI alert of 31 October 2026 (THORNode and RPC node operators, instant exchanges, DeFi services, VASPs); keep Binance hot wallets exempt. Harden developer and DevOps endpoints against recruitment-themed social engineering and trojanized repositories (including repository git hooks and editor auto-run tasks), and restrict direct access from engineer workstations to production signing services. Analytic Conclusion The 29 October 2026 compromise of Binance hot wallets is assessed as a North Korean TraderTraitor operation, as attributed by the FBI on 31 October and corroborated by TRM Labs, Chainalysis and Elliptic. Losses are reported at approximately USD 6.3–6.4 billion across ten blockchains, the largest crypto theft on record, with about USD 2.7–3.0 billion frozen and Bitcoin proceeds unfreezable. The Ethereum drain used 50 pre-signed EIP-7702 authorizations to bind a Binance hot wallet to an attacker-deployed sweeper; the other chains were drained through the compromised signing pipeline. Open questions are the delivery method on the engineer’s device and the final recovery position. This assessment may be revised as new verified information becomes available.
