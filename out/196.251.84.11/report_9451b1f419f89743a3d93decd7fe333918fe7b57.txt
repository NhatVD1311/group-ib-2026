Balonx Sistema PhaaS Mexico
[]

Executive Summary Group-IB has detected a Phishing-as-a-Service (PhaaS) operation directed at the Mexican financial sector, with a primary focus on banking institutions. This campaign utilizes fraudulent web pages that replicate legitimate banking portals to illicitly obtain user credentials and sensitive financial data. Investigations reveal that the threat actor maintains a sophisticated yet efficient infrastructure, which has supported these operations over an extended period. Consequently, this campaign poses a persistent risk to financial organizations and their clientele within Mexico. Campaign Attributions Kit Name: Balonx Sistema. Type: Phishing-as-a-Service (PhaaS). Primary Target: Banking users in Mexico. Motivation: Financial. Targeted Companies: BBVA, Citibanamex, Banorte, Santander, HSBC, Scotiabank, Banco Azteca, BanCoppel, BanBajío, Banregio, Inbursa, Invex, Afirme, Mifel, Banjército, Banco del Bienestar, Liverpool, Plata Card, Spin by Oxxo, Falabella, American Express. Reverse Shell connection of the APK. Balonx Github Repository During our investigation, we uncovered a goldmine: the Balonx GitHub repository ( hXXps://github[.]com/ferzamorafs19/xxxxxxxxxxxx31 ). A deep dive into its code history and metadata not only confirmed the identity of the main developer but also revealed the infrastructure used to build this entire PhaaS platform. The Replit Footprint: Where the Code Began Balonx wasn't built on a local machine and then pushed to GitHub. It was built and deployed entirely via Replit, a cloud-based development environment. This is a crucial finding, confirmed by several technical clues: All commit emails (except those from Gmail) use the @users[.]noreply[.]replit[.]com domain. Numeric prefixes in these emails match specific Replit user account IDs. Many commit messages are Replit's automatic saves, such as "Saved progress at the end of the loop." The Operational Takeaway: This means Replit has direct, invaluable records, including payment methods, IP addresses, and account metadata, for at least two accounts linked directly to the operator. The Operators Behind the Curtain The commit history identifies four author signatures, which boil down to two main human operators: Operator A (Primary Developer): Identified via Replit username ferzamorafs19 (ID 42753891). This operator handled the strategic, high-level structural changes like subdomain configurations, link generation logic, and session management—the brains of the operation. A security slip even exposed a real email: ferzamorafs19@gmail[.]com . Operator B (Primary Committer): Identified as greelgfs (ID 40580966) with the display name "Greto Morato." This operator accounts for about 89% of the 944 total commits. This massive volume suggests their role was bulk implementation, possibly using AI coding tools like Replit Ghostwriter or GitHub Copilot. The Temporal Fingerprint: When and Where They Work Analyzing the commit times reveals a clear, consistent pattern tied to a single geographical location: México. The quietest period for commits (12:00–17:00 UTC) perfectly aligns with 06:00–11:00 local time in México (UTC-6). The most active hours (00:00–11:00 UTC) correspond to México's evening through late night (18:00–05:00 local time). This classic pattern, combined with the kit's Mexican targets and Spanish-language commit messages, is a textbook signature for a México-based operator. Furthermore, one commit was explicitly authored with the timezone offset -0600 (CST México) , providing direct, undeniable confirmation. Domain Based Infrastructure The operational model of this fraudulent campaign involves the continuous rotation of domains to maintain the persistence of the phishing infrastructure. As established domains are deactivated or decommissioned, the threat actors migrate their operations to new portals. The default project configurations facilitated comprehensive access to all associated domains. This access enabled the verification that the most recently established domain contains data identical to that of its predecessors. Such findings indicate a unified infrastructure wherein all domains transmit and present exfiltrated information derived from a centralized database. Technical analysis of associated GitHub repositories revealed alternative default credentials; however, subsequent attempts to utilize these for authentication on the new domain were unsuccessful. Advertising structure and admin identification Service advertisement During the course of this investigation, Group-IB observed various advertisements facilitating the promotion of the PhaaS platform across numerous Facebook community groups. These digital environments, operating under nomenclature such as Base de Datos and Bases de datos Negocios Serios , serve as illicit hubs for the exchange of exfiltrated data. The participant demographic within these informal sectors primarily consists of telemarketing operatives and specialized threat actors. Our intelligence indicates that promotional content for the Balonx system is disseminated via both comments and dedicated posts. Notably, the affiliated cybercriminals frequently leverage Facebook's anonymity features to obfuscate their primary identity during these recruitment and advertising activities. Figure 21 . Direct promotion of the platform. An alternative method of promotion was detected across the previously noted groups and within individual and public Facebook profiles. In these cases, individuals disseminated abbreviated video demonstrations illustrating the operational capabilities of the phishing infrastructure. In contrast to the explicit advertisements where victims were funneled toward Telegram, these specific publications directed prospective buyers to initiate contact via direct messaging. The minimal engagement and absence of personal data on these profiles, coupled with the recurring use of a standardized tutorial video, indicates that these accounts are either fraudulent accounts managed by the SuperAdmin or affiliated operatives functioning as resellers. This discovery provides our clients with unprecedented defensive foresight by exposing the adversary's entire operational lifecycle, from initial advertisement of the PhaaS to the infrastructure deployment ending with the real-time credential exfiltration. The pivotal exploitation of information leakage, which facilitated administrative access to the platform's control panel, was instrumental in transforming this investigation; it granted us granular visibility into threat actor behavior, victim targeting, and session management that would otherwise remain opaque. Given the adversary’s demonstrated reliance on rapid domain rotation to maintain persistence, ongoing and rigorous monitoring of new infrastructure is essential to proactively neutralize future campaigns.
